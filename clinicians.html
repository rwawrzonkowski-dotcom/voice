For clinicians

For BCBAs, SLPs, RBTs and teachers. This page covers the reasoning behind the design of V.O.I.C.E. (Variable Output for Individualized Communication and Expression) and what the teaching tools do.

V.O.I.C.E. supports an individualized AAC assessment by an SLP or team. It does not replace one, and it is not speech-language therapy. It is in pilot use now, with a public release planned.

Design principles

  • Fixed motor plansWords keep their places as the board grows. New vocabulary is revealed in permanent, saved locations, never by rearranging the words a learner already knows.
  • Core vocabulary firstThe board includes all 36 Universal Core words (Project Core). The full vocabulary is about 1,700 words.
  • Reveal stagesStage 1 shows six words: help, stop, eat, drink, bathroom, more. Later stages reveal more of the same layout. See the stages.
  • Color by word typeButtons follow the Modified Fitzgerald Key convention.

What the research says

Families most often ask whether AAC will slow speech and whether their child is ready. The family answers rest on this evidence.

  • AAC and speech. A research review of AAC intervention and speech production in individuals with developmental disabilities found no decrease in speech in any of the 27 cases with adequate experimental control; 89% showed gains (Millar, Light & Schlosser, 2006). A systematic review limited to children with autism likewise found that AAC did not impede speech, and most studies reported increases, though the gains were modest (Schlosser & Wendt, 2008). Set expectations accordingly: AAC does not hold speech back and may support it, but it is not a substitute for speech-language therapy.
  • Prerequisites. Romski and Sevcik (2005) review the belief that children must show certain cognitive or sensorimotor skills before AAC is introduced and argue against excluding young children on that basis. V.O.I.C.E. has no entry requirements; Stage 1 is built for first use.

Millar, D. C., Light, J. C., & Schlosser, R. W. (2006). The impact of augmentative and alternative communication intervention on the speech production of individuals with developmental disabilities: A research review. Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research, 49(2), 248–264.
Romski, M., & Sevcik, R. A. (2005). Augmentative communication and early intervention: Myths and realities. Infants & Young Children, 18(3), 174–185.
Schlosser, R. W., & Wendt, O. (2008). Effects of augmentative and alternative communication intervention on speech production in children with autism: A systematic review. American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology, 17(3), 212–230.

Teaching sessions

Sessions are opt-in and appear in the Clinician view. Each session records who ran it (parent, RBT, teacher, BCBA or other) and where (home, clinic, school or community).

  • Targets can be single words or sentences.
  • Prompt ladders run from most help to least, then independent. The default ladder fades only the Model prompt.
  • Automatic fading applies to most-to-least targets.
  • Chaining: backward and forward chaining are built in.
  • Errorless sentence trials block incorrect buttons while the current word’s prompt is at 50% or higher. Blocked errors are logged.
  • Natural environment opportunities can be recorded alongside trials.
  • Mastery and next-stage suggestions appear at the end of a session. An adult confirms every change.

The Model prompt

The Model prompt is a ring around the target, a small model “critter,” or both (a ring only by default). The critter is Dibs the pigeon unless an adult picks one of his Chicago friends. Both fade in steps. The adult delivers the reinforcement, not the app.

In Model mode, an adult can also type a sentence and V.O.I.C.E. models it on the board word by word, along each word’s path.

Moving to the next stage

V.O.I.C.E. suggests the next stage when the learner is proficient with enough of the current one. You set the rule.

  • Proficient means a mastered target, or independent everyday use on three or more days in the last four weeks.
  • How much is enough: a percent of the stage’s main-board words (50% by default), or a list of words you choose for each stage.
  • Progress shows under Vocabulary stage in the adult menu.

Data and reports

Everyday recording runs outside sessions, so reports cover everyday use and not only trials.

  • A heat map of where on the board the learner taps, with adult modeling shown separately.
  • New words and multi-word messages over time.
  • A one-page parent summary, written in plain language, that prints cleanly.
  • Export for your own analysis.

All of it stays on the iPad unless an adult exports it. See Privacy.

Language growth

  • Grammar: a Forms button changes the last word (go to went). Pressing and holding a word opens a dial of its forms in fixed directions.
  • Little words: a strip of eight little words in permanent slots. Each one opens four related words.
  • Keyboard and number pad: the keyboard can say letter names or letter sounds. The number pad says numbers aloud.
  • Reading stage: words only, with the pictures hidden.
  • Camera scenes: an adult photographs a setting, V.O.I.C.E. suggests words, and the adult approves them. They appear in a separate strip, so core words never move.

Access

  • Display: High contrast, CVI, Color-blind safe and Dim themes. Atkinson Hyperlegible, Lexend and OpenDyslexic fonts. Larger labels and wider gaps.
  • Touch: select on lift-off, larger touch zones, keyguard mode, dwell and hold-to-select.
  • Switch scanning.
  • For the listener: a Show screen that flips the message to face them.
  • Several learners can share one iPad, each with their own board and settings.
